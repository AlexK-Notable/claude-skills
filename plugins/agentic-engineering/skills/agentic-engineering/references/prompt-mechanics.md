## T3.1 — Serial position and frontloading: what's actually known `[MEDIUM]`

The honest version of "put important things first":

- Primacy and recency biases are real and measured, but the U-shaped lost-in-the-middle effect is **conditional on window occupancy** — strong up to ~50% occupancy, dissolving into recency-dominance beyond it (full treatment: `context-degradation.md` T2.3; open-source models only, Claude-family transfer untested).
- Position is **one of three established degradation mechanisms** — lexical matching and length itself are the others, and the ranking among them is open (T2.2). The practical asymmetry stands: a fact phrased in the query's vocabulary in the middle of the window is often more findable than a paraphrased fact at the front, and neither placement nor phrasing makes a long window free.

Defensible practice: frontload instructions and identity-critical content *when operating at low occupancy* (the usual case for curated agent prompts); anchor must-survive instructions near the end as well when windows run hot; never rely on position to rescue content that lacks lexical overlap with how it will be queried.

## T3.2 — Instruction-following mechanics `[HIGH/MEDIUM — adversarial batch 2026-06-13]`

- **Dropping an instruction is an enforcement failure at generation time, not a memory failure.** The knows-but-violates rate — the model can recite the constraint on probe (~97% recall) yet violates it while generating — ranges 8% to 99% across models, and **does not track model tier in any obvious way** (GPT-5.4 8%, Gemini Pro 18%, Qwen3-235B 55%, Gemini Flash 76%, Llama-3.3-70B 93%, a Sonnet variant 99%). That last part is our reading of the table, not a result: with seven models the paper runs no correlation test and files the relationship as open `[MEDIUM — arXiv:2604.28031, single preprint, multi-turn ideation domain]`. Load-bearing consequence: **reminding a high-violation model of a constraint it already knows does not fix the drop** — re-injection treats an enforcement problem as a memory problem.
- **Constraints cost you the task, not your obedience.** Add self-evident constraints — ones the model's own unconstrained answer already satisfied — and constraint satisfaction stays flat near the ceiling (averaging over 94%) while *task* success falls (SustainScore averaging 84%). The damage is front-loaded: most of it lands within the first ~5 constraints, after which the curve flattens `[HIGH — arXiv:2601.22047]`. Two scope notes: the sweep behind that curve is eight models over 1 to 16 keyword constraints on a math task, and the plateau is what the authors use to argue that 5 constraints suffice to *measure* robustness — it is not a recommended ceiling on how many you may write. Practical rule: spend your first three-to-five constraints on what matters most, and grade the deliverable rather than the checklist. A model that ticks every box while quietly getting the answer wrong is the documented failure mode here, not a hypothetical one.
- **The failure *shape* is model-tier-dependent — but its thresholds move with each model generation.** As instruction count climbs (10–500 tested), stronger models hold near-perfect then drop sharply at a threshold, mid-tier models degrade linearly, weaker models exponentially `[MEDIUM — IFScale, arXiv:2507.11538; cite the curve taxonomy, not the absolute scores]`. The ~150-instruction cliff the benchmark is known for is a 2025 artifact: a May 2026 replication found frontier models pinned at 100% at N=500 and had to widen the keyword vocabulary from 500 to 10,000 words before any degradation reappeared, pushing the breaking point from roughly 200–300 simultaneous constraints to roughly 2,000 `[CORROBORATED* — Arize replication, 12 May 2026]`. Keep the taxonomy; treat every published instruction-count threshold as generation-specific and re-measure on the model you actually ship.
- **Constraints interfere with each other.** Opposed instructions trade off measurably — negative inter-constraint correlations of −0.28 to −0.34 between readability and forced keyword inclusion, and −0.32 to −0.44 between "avoid X" / "use X" style twins. The sharpest conflict measured is not a semantic twin at all: −0.531 between a token-count requirement and respond-in-JSON, a length budget fighting a structural format `[HIGH — MOSAIC, arXiv:2601.18554, EACL 2026, marketing-copy domain]`. Don't assume constraints compose for free; conflicting ones silently degrade each other, and format constraints count as constraints.
- **Positive exemplars beat prohibitions, with a mechanism.** Naming a forbidden token tends to *prime* it: ~87.5% of "do not say X" violations were priming failures where mentioning X activated it `[MEDIUM — single model, Qwen-2.5-7B, arXiv:2601.08070; frontier transfer untested]`. This corroborates the settled vendor guidance to show desired output rather than enumerate prohibitions, and adds the **ironic-rebound** caveat that restating a prohibition can make it worse.
- **In multi-turn work, reinforce in the *first* follow-up turn.** 74% of multi-turn instruction drift first violates by turn 2 of a 6-turn task; periodic reflection checkpoints reduced violations for some models (Qwen 55%→36%) but not others (Llama 93%→92%) `[MEDIUM — arXiv:2604.28031]`.
- **Turning on thinking redistributes instruction-following errors rather than removing them.** Across Qwen3 and Hunyuan on IFEval, the aggregate pass-rate change from built-in thinking is small (−0.55 to −3.52pp), but 10–20% of prompts flip between pass and fail: planning-type constraints (global counting, structure, coordination) improve, while precision constraints on exact local form consistently worsen `[CORROBORATED* — arXiv:2606.09662, single-author preprint, post-stamp]`. Consequence: extended thinking is not a blanket fix for a dropped format rule. It helps the constraints that need bookkeeping more than the ones that need literal compliance, so a formatting violation is a reason to move the constraint, not a reason to raise the thinking budget.

**Placement (system vs user turn) — thin evidence, not a void.** The circulating "+32/+37.4pp on Gemini" figures do have a source: arXiv:2603.25056 §4.2, a 2×2 factorial varying security framing independently in the system prompt and in the user turn. System-prompt framing moved the metric far more than user-turn framing (+32.0pp vs +0.9pp on Gemini 3 Flash; +37.4pp vs +10.6pp on Gemini 2.5 Flash). Three things stop that from being a general law of placement: the metric is phishing-detection recall for an email-triage agent, not compliance at large; it is an unreviewed preprint on one model family; and the interaction term is strongly **negative** (−27.4pp and −30.7pp), meaning security framing in *both* slots scores below what the two main effects predict — restating it in the second slot cost recall instead of adding to it `[MEDIUM — arXiv:2603.25056, preprint, security-detection domain]`. Read it as directional only: if a framing gets one good home, the system prompt is usually it, and echoing it into the user turn is not free. What *is* settled `[VENDOR-DOC]`: recent frontier models follow instructions more literally, so over-aggressive "CRITICAL: YOU MUST" language written for older models now overtriggers — dial it back to plain statements of when an action applies. This composes with T3.1: position is window-occupancy-conditional, and lexical overlap with the eventual query matters more than position.

## T3.3 / P1.5 — Structured output `[CORROBORATED*/HIGH — Opus probe 2026-06-13]`

Settled mechanics `[VENDOR-DOC]`: platform structured outputs guarantee schema-valid JSON via `output_config.format` / strict tool schemas; schema compilation is cached (~24h); constraints compose with tool use; refusals and token-limit truncation escape the guarantee. The empirical "why/when" beyond the mechanics:

- **The 2024 "structured output hurts reasoning" alarm did not replicate on a same-prompt basis.** The origin result ("Let Me Speak Freely?", EMNLP 2024) tested no frontier reasoning models and was largely attributed to methodology — different prompts per condition and a faulty AI parser; a same-prompt rerun found constrained generation *matched or beat* free-form on every matched pair (Llama-3-8B Last Letter: 0.77 vs 0.73 under the JSON prompt, 0.68 vs 0.65 under the natural-language prompt) `[CORROBORATED*, vendor-interested on both sides]`. Note the size as well as the sign: the honest constrained-vs-free delta is ~3–4 points, in constrained's favour. The ~12-point gap people quote comes from pairing the best structured run against the worst free-form one — a cross-condition comparison, which is the precise methodological error the rebuttal exists to name. Do not cite the original alarm's magnitudes as current.
- **The real, reproducible residual cost is field ordering.** Generation follows schema order, so a schema that puts the *answer field before a reasoning field* forces the model to commit before it thinks `[CORROBORATED* — mechanism matches autoregressive generation]`. **Fix (free, now consensus): declare a free-text `reasoning` field first — and mark it required.** On Anthropic, required properties are emitted before optional ones no matter where you declared them, so an *optional* `reasoning` field placed first silently lands after the answer and buys nothing; the documented remedies are to make every property required or to absorb the reordering downstream `[VENDOR-DOC]`. Nothing errors when this happens, which is why it survives review. Reasoning/thinking models largely sidestep the problem by emitting thinking tokens before the JSON block — grammar state resets between sections, so thinking is unconstrained and only the final output is schema-bound `[VENDOR-DOC]`.
- **Constraining can *raise* accuracy, not merely reliability** — JSONSchemaBench measured ~1–4 point gains over free generation across GSM8K / Last Letter / Shuffle Objects, a safety-net effect. Scope it honestly: the downstream-accuracy experiments run on one model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, itself fine-tuned for schema adherence), and the paper makes no claim that the benefit is largest on small models `[MEDIUM — arXiv:2501.10868, single model tested]`.
- **Latency is rarely the deciding factor.** Optimized engines add tens of microseconds per token (sometimes net *faster*, since constraints shorten output); the only real cost is one-time grammar compilation on complex *uncached* schemas `[HIGH]`.
- **Constrained decoding cannot signal that the input is out of schema.** Handed something the schema doesn't describe, it fills required fields with schema-valid garbage (the false-confidence failure: a banana receipt → `quantity: 1` instead of an error). The one refusal the grammar can't suppress is the platform's own — a safety refusal returns `stop_reason: "refusal"` as a normal 200 and escapes the schema entirely — but that is a safety escape hatch, not a semantic one, and it will not fire merely because your input was wrong. Pair strict schemas with an explicit `error`/refusal field or a free-text escape hatch `[CORROBORATED*, vendor]`. Relatedly, over-typing a field (e.g. `integer` where the true value is 51.7) forces a wrong answer — leave latitude where the model needs it.

**Decision rule:** constrain when the consumer is a machine and the model can't be trusted to emit valid JSON (weaker models, closed-enum extraction) — reliability gain, often an accuracy gain. Prefer free-generation-then-parse when the task needs hard reasoning you can't front-load, or when the model must be able to refuse invalid input. The `reasoning`-field-first pattern collapses most of the tradeoff for reasoning-bearing structured tasks.
