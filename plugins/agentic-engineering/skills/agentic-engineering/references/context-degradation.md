# Context Degradation: Why Long Contexts Fail

Theory pillar T2. Status: **complete**. Claims verified 2026-06-12; corrections applied 2026-08-08. **Generational scope:** most magnitudes below come from 2023–mid-2025 model generations — read them as shape, not as forecasts for the model you run.

The mid-2026 frontier is now directly measured. **ATLAS** (arXiv:2605.28079, May 2026) `[CORROBORATED*]` profiles 26 models on an 8K–1M length grid across eight capability dimensions, and its headline is that long-context decay is **capability-specific, not a single failure mode**: a model may preserve in-context learning while losing evidence retrieval, or retain structured-code behavior while failing open-document QA. Mean relative decay across the 26 models is 24.3%, spanning 8.5% (Claude Opus 4.6, the lowest) to 60.5% (GLM-4.7 non-reasoning). Retrieval and QA are the most decay-prone dimensions — multiple models lose more than 40% of their 128K score by 1M (GPT-5.2 retrieval 88.21 → 47.31, QA 74.87 → 30.51; Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview retrieval 84.96 → 49.67) — while in-context learning stays near-flat at 1M (GPT-5.2 97.96, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview 98.32). Rankings reshuffle between the 8K–128K and 8K–1M profiles, with 7 models moving at least two ranks: **a benchmark score at one length tells you little about behavior at your working length, and "long-context performance" is not one number.** The *mechanisms* below remain the durable content.

## T2.1 — Context rot: degradation is real on frontier models, and non-uniform `[HIGH]`

Across 18 frontier and open-weight models (Claude Opus 4/Sonnet 4, GPT-4.1, o3, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3, ...), retrieval performance **degrades non-uniformly as input length grows, even on tasks the same models solve reliably at short lengths** (Chroma technical report, Jul 2025; replication code public).

Key properties of the degradation:

- It is not a cliff at the context limit — it accumulates well inside nominal capacity.
- It is task-difficulty-dependent: simple lexical-match retrieval survives long contexts far better than ambiguous or inference-requiring retrieval. This is why **vanilla needle-in-a-haystack scores systematically overstate robustness** for realistic queries.
- Source caveat: Chroma is a retrieval vendor with a commercial stake in the "context engineering > big windows" narrative; the report is not peer-reviewed and uses mostly synthetic tasks. Its similarity-dependence finding, however, is independently corroborated by peer-reviewed work (NoLiMa, below).

**Design consequence:** budget context as a depreciating asset. Tokens late in a large window buy less reliable attention than the same tokens in a small window. "It fits in context" is not the bar; "it will be *found* in context" is.

## T2.2 — Mechanisms: lexical matching, position, and length itself `[HIGH for measurements, MEDIUM for mechanism attribution]`

NoLiMa (LMU Munich + Adobe Research, ICML 2025) is the sharpest evidence on *why* long-context retrieval fails. It builds a needle set where question and needle share minimal lexical overlap, forcing models to infer latent associations rather than pattern-match surface forms — and performance collapses. The paper's own causal reading is appropriately hedged: "Our analysis suggests these declines stem from the increased difficulty the attention mechanism faces in longer contexts when literal matches are absent, making it harder to retrieve relevant information." Take that as strongly indicated, not proven.

Measured on 2024-generation models at only 32K tokens (far inside claimed 128K limits): GPT-4o drops 99.3% → 69.7% accuracy on non-literal-match retrieval; Llama 3.3 70B drops 97.3% → 42.7%. Of the 13 models evaluated, **eleven fell below 50% of their strong short-length baselines at 32K** (GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro were the two that stayed above). Effective context lengths implied: ~8K for GPT-4o, ~2K for Llama 3.3 70B — versus 128K claimed.

The maintained repo has since added GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5, Llama 4, and o3/o4-mini. **Newer generation has not meant uniform improvement.** GPT-4.1 genuinely improved (79.8 at 32K, effective length 16K against GPT-4o's 8K), and on the harder NoLiMa-Hard reasoning table o3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro lead at 32K (58.5 from a 100.0 base; 58.6 from 99.1) — but reasoning is no blanket fix there, with o1 at 31.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash w/ thinking at 15.7, and o4-mini at 11.7. Llama 4 Maverick *regressed* against the Llama 3.3 70B it succeeds — 24.5 at 32K versus 42.7 — Llama 4 Scout's effective length is 1K against a claimed 10M, and GPT-4.1 Mini and Nano both land under 1K effective. A bigger claimed window and a newer checkpoint are not evidence of anything here.

**Three mechanisms, not one.** The folk model is purely positional ("things in the middle get lost"). The literature supports at least three distinct causes, and they stack rather than compete:

1. **Lexical.** Retrieval succeeds when the query's surface forms collide with the target's, and degrades with length because more text means more competing near-matches and weaker signal per token (NoLiMa). Mechanistic follow-up localizes this: LOCOS (arXiv:2607.01002, Jul 2026) `[CORROBORATED*]` identifies *non-literal* retrieval heads by what they write through their output-value circuits rather than what they attend to — ablating the top 50 in Qwen3-8B collapses NoLiMa retrieval from 0.401 to 0.000 ROUGE-L while parametric recall and arithmetic reasoning stay at baseline.
2. **Positional.** Primacy and recency biases are real and measurable (T2.3), and there is now theory arguing they are architectural rather than learned: an exact analysis of multi-layer causal attention (arXiv:2603.10123, Mar 2026) `[MEDIUM]` — single-author preprint — derives the U-shape from causal masking plus residual connections and reports it already present at initialization in untrained Qwen2 and GPT-2, with or without RoPE.
3. **Length itself.** *Context Length Alone Hurts LLM Performance Despite Perfect Retrieval* (arXiv:2510.05381, Findings of EMNLP 2025) holds retrieval constant and still measures 13.9–85% degradation. The drop survives replacing irrelevant tokens with whitespace, masking them out entirely, and placing all supporting evidence immediately before the question. Levy et al., *Same Task, More Tokens* (ACL 2024), found the same shape earlier on padded-but-identical tasks: accuracy 0.92 → 0.68 by roughly 3,000 tokens, far below any model's claimed maximum.

The third is the uncomfortable one, because it bounds what curation can buy: **a perfectly curated long window still underperforms a short one on the same task.** "Engineer the context better" reduces the tax; it does not make length free. Which mechanism dominates in a given failure is not settled — treat the ranking among the three as open, and the existence of all three as established.

**Design consequences:**
1. When you control both the query and the stored content (memory files, notes, tool results you format), **engineer lexical overlap deliberately** — consistent terminology, IDs repeated verbatim, headers that echo the questions agents will ask.
2. Paraphrase-heavy contexts (summaries that rename things, synonyms for variety) actively damage retrievability.
3. NIAH-style "we tested our context window" claims tell you almost nothing about performance on real queries.
4. Because length alone costs you something, a short window is a design goal, not a fallback. Where you cannot shorten, the length-alone paper's model-agnostic mitigation is cheap to try: have the model recite the retrieved evidence before reasoning over it (up to +4% for GPT-4o on RULER).

## T2.3 — Lost-in-the-middle, revised: conditional on window occupancy `[MEDIUM]`

The 2023/24 lost-in-the-middle finding (U-shaped accuracy by position: strong at start and end, weak in the middle) was substantially revised in 2025 (COLM 2025, arXiv:2508.07479): measured by input length **relative to each model's context window**, the U-shape is strongest when inputs occupy up to ~50% of the window. Beyond ~50% occupancy, primacy bias (the frontloading advantage) weakens while recency bias stays stable — the U-curve dissolves into a distance-based bias favoring information near the end. The paper attributes prior contradictory LiM replications to studies using absolute rather than relative input lengths; the ~50% threshold held across all six models tested.

**Why only MEDIUM:** single paper; open-source models only (Llama-3.x 70B, Mistral-Small-24B, Qwen-2.5-32B, Gemma-2-27B; 8K–128K windows); largely synthetic tasks. The authors used open-weight models exclusively on cost grounds — their stated figure is roughly US$10,000 to run the full experiment set on GPT-4 Turbo (128K), against about 24 hours on four H100s for Llama 3.1 70B — so no closed-source model was tested, not merely Claude and GPT. **Transfer to Claude-family 200K–1M windows is an untested extrapolation — flag it whenever you apply this.**

**Design consequence:** "frontload the important content" is *conditional* advice. At low window occupancy (the common case for well-curated agent contexts) frontloading exploits intact primacy bias. At high occupancy, primacy decays — the end of the window becomes the only reliably privileged position, which is one reason recency-anchored patterns (instructions repeated near the end, freshest tool results last) survive in practice. If you operate near window capacity, do not assume your system-prompt-position content is being attended to.

## T2.4 — Claimed vs effective context (compressed history) `[HIGH, historical]`

RULER (NVIDIA, COLM 2024) established the claimed-vs-effective gap: nearly all of 17 evaluated models scored near-perfectly on vanilla NIAH yet showed large drops on harder tasks as length grew; though all claimed ≥32K contexts, only about half performed satisfactorily at 32K. Those numbers are 2023–24-era — treat the specific statistic as historical.

HELMET (ICLR 2025) sharpens RULER rather than retiring it, and the distinction matters. Its criticism targets the **RULER average score** as a predictor of real-world performance: that average mixes NIAH variants with synthetic aggregation, multi-hop tracing, and QA, and correlates with downstream tasks at below 0.85 — no synthetic task in HELMET's comparison clears 0.8 on average. Its *harder* tasks HELMET keeps: several RULER tasks are folded into HELMET's own synthetic-recall set, and RULER MK — which places distracting needles around the target — is called out as differentiating models better than vanilla NIAH, because noisier, more distracting contexts separate models that plain retrieval cannot. The durable lesson, corroborated by NoLiMa and Chroma on newer models: **"effective context length" is the operative concept, it is task-dependent, and it is always shorter than the spec sheet.**

## T2.5 — Distractors, and how models fail differently `[HIGH, family-rankings MEDIUM]`

Distractor content — plausible but wrong passages sharing the window with the target — **amplifies long-context failure non-uniformly**, and the effect is multi-sourced rather than a single vendor's observation. Chroma (2025) measured it across 18 models. RULER's multi-key variants insert 4 distractor needles (or fill the haystack with them outright) and show retrieval falling steadily as distracting needles are added, with Yi dropping ~40 points at 256K in the extreme version. NoLiMa's own ablation inserts a single sentence carrying the query keyword but unrelated to the needle, and that alone cuts GPT-4o's effective length from 8K to 1K, with Llama 3.3 70B worse.

Levy et al., *Same Task, More Tokens* (ACL 2024), supplies the honest complication: comparing padding drawn from similar in-domain instances against unrelated Books-Corpus text, the drop from the *unrelated* padding was mostly the larger of the two. Semantic similarity to the query is therefore not the whole story — some of what looks like distraction is length doing the work (T2.2, mechanism 3).

The failure *signature* under distractors is family-specific, and here the evidence is single-source (Chroma, mid-2025 models): Claude Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 showed the lowest hallucination rates, tending to abstain when uncertain and explicitly stating that no answer can be found, while GPT models showed the highest, tending to generate confident but incorrect responses drawn from the distractor. That ranking may not transfer to mid-2026 models; the structural points do:

1. Distractor sensitivity means context *pollution* is a distinct failure axis from context *length*. A short, polluted window can underperform a long, clean one. Curation (removing stale tool results, near-duplicate retrievals, superseded drafts) is not cosmetic — and *which* tokens you drop matters more than how many. A distractor-aware truncation study (arXiv:2608.03297, Aug 2026) `[CORROBORATED*]`, a single-author preprint, makes the point by inversion: naive middle-removal degrades monotonically as retention falls, but under it the answer-bearing content survives in fewer than 1% of samples at 25% retention — so the experiment was measuring accidental deletion of the answer, not a context-length effect. Signal-preserving truncation held performance flat or improved it.
2. Abstention and hallucination are different downstream risks requiring different harness responses: abstention-prone models need retry/escalation paths; hallucination-prone models need verification gates. Know which signature your model exhibits *under your workload* before designing the recovery path.
3. **The obvious fix is not free.** Adding a "don't make it up" instruction pushes models toward over-conservative refusal of facts that *are* present — a safety tax. Across four models, ChatGPT-5-mini's literal extraction at capacity fell 89.0% → 72.0% under the anti-hallucination prompt and its inference accuracy 88% → 68%, while DeepSeek-v3.2-chat barely moved (99% → 97%) (arXiv:2601.02023, rev. Jul 2026) `[CORROBORATED*]`. The cost is model-dependent, which means measuring it on your model, not assuming your guardrail prompt is costless.
4. **Long context also makes agents quit early.** A long-horizon search study (arXiv:2606.29718, Jun 2026) `[CORROBORATED*]` identifies *premature termination* — giving up, or returning uncertain incorrect answers, long before the context window is exhausted — and finds its rate rises with context length after controlling for query difficulty. Context management functions there as a test-time scaling lever that buys more exploration (+2.6–4.9% across three aggregation methods with behavior-aware filtering). When an agent stops short, suspect its window before rewriting its stop condition.
